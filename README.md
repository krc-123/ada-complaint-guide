# Writing an ADA Complaint That Gets Read

A plain-language guide for families and individuals. I help people write these through my work with the American Society for Deaf Children, usually after a school, clinic, business, or government office has refused an interpreter, ignored a captioning request, or made a building or website impossible to use.

I am a registered nurse and a legal nurse consultant. I am not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice. If a deadline is close or the harm is serious, talk to a disability rights attorney or your state's Protection and Advocacy agency. Both are free to consult in most cases.

## First, work out which door you are knocking on

The Americans with Disabilities Act has several parts, and complaints go to different places depending on who did the thing.

- **A state or local government body** (public school, county clinic, DMV, court, public library, city website): that is ADA Title II. Complaints go to the U.S. Department of Justice through its civil rights complaint portal, or to the federal agency that funds that service (for public schools, the Department of Education Office for Civil Rights).
- **A business open to the public** (private clinic, hospital, pharmacy, restaurant, hotel, store, private school, a company's website or app): that is ADA Title III. Complaints go to the Department of Justice. You can also sue directly, and many people do.
- **An employer** (hiring, accommodations at work, firing): that is ADA Title I. Complaints go to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), and the deadline is short, 180 days from the event in most states, 300 in some.
- **Anything that receives federal money** (most hospitals, Medicaid providers, universities, Head Start): Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act also applies, and you can complain to that agency's Office for Civil Rights. Health care providers also fall under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, complaints to HHS Office for Civil Rights.

One event can fit more than one door. A public hospital that refused an ASL interpreter is Title II, Section 504, and Section 1557 all at once. File with the one most likely to act quickly, and say in the complaint that you are aware the others apply.

Check deadlines the day you decide to complain. Office for Civil Rights complaints generally need to be filed within 180 days of the event. The Department of Justice does not have a fixed deadline for Title II and III complaints but prioritizes recent ones.

## What a complaint needs

Investigators read hundreds of these. The ones that move are short, specific, dated, and ask for something concrete.

1. **Who you are and who you are complaining about.** Full names, addresses, phone numbers, and the name of the organization and the specific location or website.
2. **The disability, in one sentence.** "My son is Deaf and communicates in American Sign Language." You do not need medical records in the complaint.
3. **What happened, in order, with dates.** Each event on its own line. Date, place, who you spoke to, what you asked for, what they said. Quote them if you can. "On 14 March 2026 at 2:10 pm, the front desk clerk, Ms. Patel, said the clinic does not provide interpreters and that I should bring a family member."
4. **What you asked for and when.** Interpreter, captioning, accessible PDF, ramp, extra time, a different format. Show that you asked clearly and gave them a chance.
5. **What it cost you.** Missed appointment, misunderstood diagnosis, child sent home from school, could not register for a class, could not pay a bill online. Be factual. Investigators are moved by consequence more than by adjectives.
6. **What you want.** This is the part most people leave out. Say it: "I want the clinic to adopt a written policy for providing qualified ASL interpreters within 24 hours of request, to train front desk staff, and to reschedule my appointment with an interpreter at no cost."
7. **Proof.** Attach emails, texts, letters, photos, screenshots with the date visible, names of witnesses. If a website is the problem, attach a screenshot with the URL and date, and name the barrier precisely ("the appointment form cannot be completed with a screen reader; the date field has no label").

## Before you file

- Put the request in writing to the organization first, if it is safe to do so, and keep the reply or the silence. A complaint that shows you asked twice and were ignored is far stronger than one that shows you went straight to a federal agency.
- Keep one folder, paper or digital, with everything in date order. Investigators will ask for it.
- Write the complaint, put it away for a night, and reread it. Cut anything that is about how you feel and keep everything that is about what happened.
- Ask someone outside the situation to read it and tell you what they think you are asking for. If they cannot say it in one sentence, rewrite section 6.

## After you file

You will get an acknowledgment. Then, often, a long silence. That is normal. Keep a note of the complaint number and the date. If you have not heard anything in 60 days, write a short polite follow-up quoting the complaint number. Keep documenting any new incidents; they can be added.

If the organization fixes the problem before the investigation finishes, you can tell the agency so. That is a win, and it still goes on the record.

## Where to file

- U.S. Department of Justice civil rights complaint portal: https://civilrights.justice.gov/
- U.S. Department of Education Office for Civil Rights: https://www.ed.gov/ocr
- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights: https://www.hhs.gov/ocr
- Equal Employment Opportunity Commission: https://www.eeoc.gov/
- Your state's Protection and Advocacy agency (search "protection and advocacy" plus your state)
